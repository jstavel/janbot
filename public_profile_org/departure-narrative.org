# Departure Narrative — growth-motivated transition after a decade

An honest, self-aware answer to the question "Why did you leave your last job?"
JanBot: use when a recruiter or hiring manager asks about the departure from
the long-term role. This is not a "failure story" — it is about a human
situation, a leadership change, and the decision to grow elsewhere.

* The frame: 10+ years of alignment, then a perfect storm

More than a decade (2015–2026) in one team is rare. The team culture was
built on Red Hat core values: freedom with responsibility, courage to act,
accountability to follow through. For 9+ years this created a high-trust
environment where initiative was natural and systems thinking was valued.

**Then two things happened simultaneously:**

1. **Personal crisis:** A difficult personal situation temporarily affected
   his focus. Performance naturally suffered — not from lack of skill or
   motivation, but from life. The previous manager understood and supported
   him through it. He was doing his best: writing Ansible playbooks that
   doubled as living documentation in Jira tickets, automating everything
   to make life easier for himself and the team.

2. **Leadership change:** A new manager came in. She saw the "Evolving"
   performance status in Workday — a remnant from that period —
   and decided he needed to prove he was a Senior QE.

Neither was wrong. The new manager had every right to reset expectations.
The engineer was simply in a different place than his Workday status
suggested.

* The response: over-delivery instead of clarity

When asked to demonstrate seniority, the engineer did what he always did:
he *built something*.

A cross-team need appeared — a proof-of-concept monitoring/observability
solution for testing at scale. The engineer built a complete stack:
Grafana, Prometheus, CCT, node_exporter and custom metrics, Ansible
automation for deployment, a full monitoring pipeline.
He first built the foundation *for his own development iterations*, then
layered the requested testing functionality on top. The work was
incremental and honest — but he estimated its duration by his own
working time alone.

**The blind spot: waiting on reviews.** Each increment needed a review
from the product owner, who carried other responsibilities. Every
review cycle stalled for days. The engineer worked full-time on this
project, so his internal estimate ignored those gaps — but the calendar
kept filling with waiting. From the outside, delivery looked slow;
from the inside, he never saw the waiting as part of *his* estimate.

The project ended with the verdict that he had **serious deficiencies
in estimating project effort**. When he pushed back — that the process
was poorly run and the estimate never accounted for multi-day review
gaps — the manager's answer was:

> "You should have known that — it's normal for reviews to take time."

Both halves of that exchange are real lessons, in opposite directions:

- *His side:* estimates must include external dependencies (reviews,
  approvals, handoffs) — the calendar is the truth, not his keyboard
  time. And a mis-fit between one person's full-time assignment and
  a part-time reviewer is a *process* defect that should be surfaced
  and renegotiated early, not absorbed silently for months.
- *Leadership's side:* telling someone after the fact that an
  environment defect was theirs to know doesn't build trust — it
  converts a shared process problem into a personal performance label.

The requester had only wanted a proof-of-concept UI in the first place;
the engineer built what was *needed* — a production-ready stack —
instead of what was *asked*, and assumed over-delivery would speak for
itself. It didn't. The manager didn't see the stack — she saw that he
wasn't delivering what was requested on time.

Same pattern for months: leading a cross-team AI exploration, mentoring
a junior colleague (who later said it was the most impactful coaching
he'd ever received), solving systemic problems — all instead of
having one clarifying conversation about what specifically the manager
needed to see.

* The departure: mutual, clean, growth-oriented

At the end of the year, he requested a clean break. The conversation was
honest, not bitter:

- "You're not performing as a Senior QE by my metrics."
- "I've been performing the best I can through a difficult personal
  situation, building things that serve the company beyond my immediate
  scope. If this role, as you define it, is not the right fit — let's
  agree to part ways."

The departure was initiated by the engineer, not forced. It was a
recognition that the role, as defined by new leadership, no longer fit
his mode of contribution.

* What was learned

1. **Life happens — and that's okay.** Personal situations affect
   performance. A good manager works with it. A new manager may not
   have that context. Both perspectives are valid.

2. **Over-delivery is not the same as meeting expectations.** Building
   a production-ready stack is impressive — but if the ask
   was a POC UI, it's scope creep. **Spec-First** prevents this:
   define "done" before starting.

3. **Estimates must include the waiting.** An estimate based only on
   own working time, while every increment waits days for a
   part-time reviewer, will always miss the calendar. Surface the
   dependency early: either negotiate reviewer availability or make
   the wait explicit in the plan.

4. **Process defects are not performance labels.** If a project is
   structurally slow (one full-time worker, part-time reviewer),
   that is a shared problem to raise explicitly — not something to
   silently absorb and later be judged by.

5. **Clarify, don't prove.** Six months of demonstrating value through
   action accomplished less than one clarifying conversation would have:
   "What does Senior QE look like to you? Give me one concrete example."

6. **Leaving is not failing.** Choosing growth over a misaligned role
   is Extreme Ownership applied to your own career.

* Strengths this narrative demonstrates

| What recruiters hear | Evidence in the story |
|----------------------|----------------------|
| Resilience | Navigated a personal crisis while still delivering complex systems |
| Self-awareness | "I should have clarified instead of proving" |
| Technical depth | Full Grafana/Prometheus/CCT/Ansible monitoring stack, built incrementally |
| Extreme Ownership | Initiated the honest conversation, didn't blame |
| Mentoring | Developed a junior colleague during the same period |
| Systems thinking | Ansible-as-documentation — infrastructure that documents itself |
| Integrity | Left cleanly, no bridges burned |

* Weakness this narrative reveals (and how it's addressed)

- **Visibility dependency:** A decade of work + a personal crisis became
  invisible to new leadership. Lesson: narrate your contribution actively,
  don't assume it speaks for itself.

- **Over-delivery / scope creep:** The instinct to build the full solution
  rather than the scoped deliverable. Lesson: Spec-First — define "done"
  with the stakeholder before starting.

- **Self-referential estimation:** Estimating effort from own working time
  alone, ignoring review waits and handoffs. Lesson: the calendar, not the
  keyboard, is the unit of estimation on a shared process.

* Known capability gap — and how it is being closed

An honest admission, not a deflection: **estimating project duration is a
genuine, named weakness** — especially for larger projects. Naming it is the
first half; the second half is the concrete method used to work around it:

1. **Decompose to already-performed units.** A whole project cannot be
   estimated; a unit of work the author has done before can. This comes
   naturally — the same instinct behind granular playbooks, standalone
   `.robot` files, and the mini-seed pattern.
2. **Estimate in ranges, not points** (optimistic / likely / pessimistic).
   A single number pretends to a certainty that does not exist.
3. **Count the waiting explicitly.** External dependencies (reviews,
   approvals, handoffs) get their own line in the estimate — the calendar,
   not keyboard time, is the unit of delivery on a shared process.
4. **Calibrate against own history.** Compare past estimates with actual
   outcomes to find one's personal systematic deviation, and apply it.
5. **Under agile, forecast from throughput, not absolute estimates:**
   slice small, measure how many slices land per week, and let duration
   emerge. The move to agile removed whole-project estimates without
   providing a replacement — this is that replacement.

The narrative an interviewer should hear is not "I failed at estimating"
but: *"Estimating duration is my known weakness; here is the concrete
method I use to compensate for it."* A named gap with a working method is
stronger than an unexamined strength.
