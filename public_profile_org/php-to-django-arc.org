# From patching legacy PHP to server-rendered components — one web era in review

Use when asked about the web-development years, about legacy code, or about
"what did you learn from the era before frameworks". The arc has three stages,
and the honest part is that the architectural understanding came with the
framework, not with the years.

* Stage 1 — PHP, delivery by iteration

As an independent contractor he took over and extended an **information system
for a law firm, written in PHP**. The system was complicated; he extended it
module by module, followed the debug output in the browser, and iterated until it
worked. The client was satisfied.

The honest limit, stated by him: **he never got the architect's view.** There was
no moment of "now I understand the core principles", and to this day he does not
fully know why that system was written the way it was. He *carried* the complexity
without ever fully *seeing* it.

Around the same period: WordPress — templates, hosting configuration,
`.htaccess`, debugging other people's installations. High uncertainty each time,
usually ending well, always by iteration rather than by model.

One seed worth keeping: he used `array_map` and `array_filter` instead of `for`
loops instinctively. It made the code shorter and the mental model simpler — a
functional instinct years before it had a name in his toolchain
(see [[id:20261007-perl-clojure-gateway][the Perl→Clojure gateway]]).

* Stage 2 — Django 0.98: the admin as a finished CRUD environment

Then came Django **0.98**, and the discovery that a complete create/read/update/
delete environment already existed: the Django admin. It became the core of his
intranet-style projects, including [[id:20261007-cdbazar][cdbazar]], and he pushed
its extension points as far as they went.

He also learned its limits the hard way: a filter over many rows **without
pagination** produced a request that appeared to hang for ten minutes. Lesson
taken: use a framework's standard parts deliberately, not maximally.

His standing conclusion, still held: if he had to build an intranet **tomorrow**,
he would start with the Django admin.

* Stage 3 — KSS + AJAX: the page as independent fragments (pre-SPA)

On the same project, when AJAX appeared, he combined it with an idea from the
Plone world: **KSS (Kinetic Style Sheets)**, where a block of a page is declared
dynamic, rendered on the server by a specific view.

The resulting design:

- the page is split into **independent fragments**, each with its own template,
  its own view and its own refresh cycle (e.g. a shopping-cart widget showing the
  order count);
- an action renders **only the fragment affected by it** on the server;
- that HTML comes back over AJAX and replaces the fragment's content
  (`innerHTML` replace) on the client.

It behaved like a single-page application **before SPA existed**, and the goal was
explicit: do not re-render the whole page for a partial change. The architecture
kept server-side rendering while getting the interaction feel of a client-side
app — the same trade-off that decades later returns as islands, htmx and
server components.

* What the arc says

- **Delivery first, architecture later.** The PHP years prove he could keep a
  business system running and growing without a full model of it; the Django years
  prove that when a coherent structure was available he recognised it immediately
  and used it.
- **Component boundaries were an instinct before they were a framework.** The KSS
  fragment design is the same idea as today's component architecture, arrived at
  from the server side.
- **Honest limitation.** He is not the person who will reverse-engineer and
  re-architect a legacy system for its own sake; he is the person who will keep it
  delivering and reach for structure when the tooling provides one.
