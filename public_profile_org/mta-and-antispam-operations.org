# Mail plumbing as a routine — MTA eras, ClamAV + SpamAssassin, and teaching users to train their own filter

Use when asked about running real services, about email infrastructure, or about
"how do you get other people to adopt something". The interesting part is not
which MTA won; it is that a mail-security pipeline became a **routine**, and then
a **capability handed to users**.

* Three MTA eras — and only one of them had a reason

| Era | Configuration model | What it cost him |
|---|---|---|
| **Sendmail / Qmail** | Sendmail's monolithic `sendmail.cf`; Qmail as the alternative | spam waves meant the admins sat down and cleaned queues by hand |
| **Exim** | advertised as **curing Sendmail's problems through modularity** | the modular config *was* considerably simpler — and yet **it took him a long time to get into it** |
| **Postfix** | modularity **within a few files**, each with an obvious role | the one that finally stuck — and it "just smoothly started being used" |

That last line is worth keeping honest: **there was no decision.** Postfix drifted
in. Infrastructure adoption frequently works like that — a tool enters through a
colleague, a distribution default or a project need, and the rationalisation comes
afterwards. Naming it as drift, rather than inventing a comparison study, is the
accurate record.

* The modularity lesson — it is not about the number of files

Exim was the modular one, and it was still slow to learn. Postfix was also
modular, and it was quick. The difference is not file count:

- Exim's modularity **moved** the complexity into a configuration language you
  still had to learn as a whole.
- Postfix's split matched **roles he already had in his head**: something accepts,
  something queues, something delivers. Fewer concepts to hold, not fewer lines.

Stated as a rule: **the value of modularity is measured by the mental model it
removes, not by how many files it produces.** The same criterion appears
elsewhere in this corpus — `array_map` instead of a `for` loop
([[id:20261007-perl-clojure-gateway][the functional seed]]), the Django admin
instead of writing CRUD by hand
([[id:20261017-php-to-django-arc][the Django era]]), a mini-seed instead of
reseeding the whole job stack
([[id:20261008-cicd-jenkins-openshift][small blast radius]]). In every case the
question is *what does this take off my plate*, not *how sophisticated is it*.

* What actually became a skill — the mail security pipeline

Antispam and antivirus were not one-off configurations; they were **installed
repeatedly, as routine**:

- **ClamAV** for viruses and **SpamAssassin** for spam, both wired into the
  **Postfix** pipeline (the Milter path).
- **SpamAssassin** is Perl-based and **Bayesian**: `sa-learn` is fed examples of
  ham and of spam, and the filter's scoring improves from them.
- The repetition is the point. Anyone can install a package once; the competence
  is in the pipeline that survives upgrades, new users and changing traffic.

* The throughput decision — a filtering daemon between two MTA instances

The antispam was not bolted on as a per-message script. It ran as a **service**,
and the reason is a calculation rather than a taste: starting a Perl interpreter
and loading the filter's rules **for every single message** is expensive, and cost
is multiplied by volume. Running the filter as a resident daemon **amortises that
fixed cost across all traffic**.

Two components carried that reasoning, and he tried the first before choosing the
second:

- **`spamd` + `spamc` first** — SpamAssassin as a resident daemon, each message
  handled by a small client that talks to it over a socket. It works, and it
  already removes the per-message Perl start.
- **`amavisd-new` in the end** — a **resident Perl process that loads the
  SpamAssassin engine into itself** (`Mail::SpamAssassin`) and reaches the virus
  scanner as a daemon too. Even the client/server hop disappears: one long-lived
  process holds both engines warm, and the per-message Perl start is gone
  entirely.

Where it plugged in is the part he remembers as *"part of the qmgr"* — correctly:
**the Postfix queue manager invokes the content filter**, handing each message to
`amavisd-new`, which scans it and **reinjects** the result back into Postfix for
delivery. So the filter is a stage inside Postfix's own queue machinery, not a
wrapper around the whole server:

```
instance A (accepts)  →  qmgr  →  amavisd-new (resident: SpamAssassin + clamd)  →  reinject  →  instance B (delivers)
```

The two chained instances were his deliberate choice: mail handed from one to the
next, each side with its own queue and its own policy — the front end faces the
internet, the back end knows the local users.

**One term to keep straight**, because it changes who gets the credit:

- Postfix was **never "split into two processes"** — it is *by design* a pipeline
  of many small daemons, each doing exactly one thing (`master.cf` defines the
  daemons; `main.cf` the parameters). That split is the software's architecture,
  not a decision of his.
- What **was** his decision is the **two MTA instances** chained around the filter
  — the documented way to run a content filter without making the same instance
  both the front door and the delivery agent.
- And there is a third reading worth naming, because it may be the truest one:
  **the reinjection hop itself feels like two MTAs.** Mail goes out of the queue
  manager into `amavisd-new` (port 10024) and comes back in through a **second
  submission listener** (10025) before being delivered. Two listening ports, mail
  arriving twice, handed from one side to the other — which is exactly what "two
  MTAs passing mail to each other" describes, even when it is one Postfix
  installation with a content filter.

So the memory is right at the level that matters (mail handed between two MTAs,
filter resident because Perl is expensive per message) and loose in one label
(processes vs instances).

**Why it is evidence worth keeping:** it is **capacity reasoning**, not
configuration trivia — fixed cost ÷ volume, and a queue boundary drawn where the
policies differ. The same instinct as keeping a warm connection pool instead of
reconnecting, and the same instinct as loading a model once rather than per
request.

* The part he enjoyed most — handing the training loop to users

The filter was not tuned only by the administrator. Users **kept their spam in a
folder**, and their own antispam learned from that folder; he **taught them how to
train it themselves**.

That is a different kind of work from operating mail servers, and it has three
quiet requirements:

1. **A convention that survives without him** — a predictable place to put the
   spam, so the loop keeps running when he is not there.
2. **An explanation short enough to be repeated** — the user must understand *why*
   moving mail into that folder helps, or the loop stalls.
3. **Willingness to give away the control** — the tuning stops being the admin's
   private craft and becomes a shared capability.

The outcome is the same shape as the best work in this corpus: build the tool
around how people actually work, then make them able to operate it
([[id:20261007-intranet-pattern][intranet pattern]],
[[id:20261007-mentoring-downchain][mentoring down-chain]]).

* Why this is more than nostalgia about mail servers

- **Training on a labelled corpus is the same shape as everything else he does
  now.** Ham and spam in two folders is a labelled dataset; `sa-learn` improves a
  measurable score from it. That is the same instrument as the JanBot golden
  dataset and its eval gate
  ([[id:20261007-janbot-ai-agent][JanBot]],
  [[id:20261015-janbot-value-proposition][what the eval is for]]) and as Reactive
  Testing's evidence-first verification
  ([[id:20261007-reactive-testing-origin][Reactive Testing]]). Twenty years apart,
  same move: **turn a felt judgement into data you can measure against.**
- **The user-facing half is the harder half.** Installing the filter is
  engineering; getting people to keep feeding it is product and teaching work.
- **He enjoyed it because of the training set** — the pleasure was in the loop
  closing, not in the configuration files.

* Honest limits

- **Dates are relative, not pinned.** The Exim period sits before SpamAssassin
  existed (first release April 2001), which is consistent with his own reporting
  that he never put antispam into Exim — there was nothing to install yet, in his
  setup.
- **`SA-Exim` existed** (SpamAssassin shipped an Exim integration), so the absence
  of antispam in Exim is evidence about *timing and path*, not about possibility.
- **Postfix's arrival had no deliberate reason.** Recorded as drift.
- **The filter stack is remembered at last** — `spamd`+`spamc` tried first,
  `amavisd-new` chosen in the end, hooked in through the Postfix queue manager.
  What is *not* remembered are version numbers and the exact `master.cf` lines;
  the object claims the arrangement, not the configuration text.
- No claim is made here about scale (users, volume) — that detail is not
  remembered and is not needed for the lesson.
