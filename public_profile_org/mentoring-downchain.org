# Extreme Ownership DOWN — mentoring with measurable impact

Concrete evidence of the "Extreme Ownership DOWN" principle: a junior
colleague credits mentorship with creating the environment and confidence
that led to their promotion from ASME to SME. Use when a recruiter asks about
leadership, mentoring, or creating growth space for others.

* The principle

Extreme Ownership (Jocko Willink) flows in two directions:

- **UP:** Ensure your manager understands what you are doing — clean results,
  clear communication, no surprises.
- **DOWN:** Take responsibility for the growth environment of people you
  mentor. If their mind is bored, it is your responsibility to prepare a
  stimulating environment for them.

The DOWN direction is often overlooked. This object documents what it looks
like in practice.

* The evidence

Part of a formal company mentoring program at Red Hat. A mentee from India
was assigned — at the time an Associate Software Maintenance Engineer (ASME).
provided written feedback after a period of mentorship (June 2025):

> "You always made space for my questions, no matter how small or broad,
> and I really appreciated your way of listening and guiding without
> being judgmental."

> "What I liked most was that it never felt like a typical 'formal'
> mentorship. It was open, easy-going, and very real."

> "Your mentorship gave me the right direction and confidence throughout
> — it played a big role in my recent promotion from ASME to SME."

> "Thank you for being there 🙌 I'd always consider you as my mentor
> going forward too."

**The measurable outcome:** The person was promoted from Associate (ASME) to
full Subject Matter Expert (SME) during or shortly after the mentorship
period. The feedback explicitly credits the mentorship as a contributing
factor.

* What it proves

| Claim | Evidence |
|-------|----------|
| Mentorship produces measurable results | Direct quote: "played a big role in my recent promotion" |
| Psychological safety is deliberately created | "listening and guiding without being judgmental" |
| Mentorship can feel organic, not transactional | "never felt like a typical formal mentorship" |
| Cross-cultural collaboration | Assigned mentee from India, mentor based in EU |
| The relationship outlasts the formal program | "I'd always consider you as my mentor going forward" |

* The approach

The mentorship was not structured as a formal program with milestones and
deliverables. The approach was:

1. **Create space.** Make it clear that no question is too small or too broad.
2. **Listen first.** Understand where the person is before offering direction.
3. **Guide, don't instruct.** Help the person discover their own answers
   rather than providing ready-made solutions.
4. **Be real.** Drop the formal mentor persona — the relationship works because
   it is genuine, not because of a title.
5. **Own the outcome.** If the person is not growing, it is the mentor's
   responsibility to adjust the environment, not the mentee's fault.

* Strengths this demonstrates

- **Concrete leadership impact.** Not "I am a mentor" — "someone was promoted
  and credits my mentorship as a contributing factor."
- **Psychological safety.** Creating an environment where questions are safe
  is a deliberate skill, not a personality trait.
- **Cross-cultural collaboration.** Successfully mentored someone in a different
  country (assigned from India, mentor in EU), navigating time zones and
  communication styles.
- **Long-term relationship.** The mentee explicitly stated they would continue
  to consider the mentor their mentor beyond the formal program.

* Weaknesses this reveals

- **Single data point.** One mentee's experience — powerful but not a pattern
  until replicated.
- **Internal impact only.** The impact was within a single organisation;
  external portability depends on the recruiter's context.
