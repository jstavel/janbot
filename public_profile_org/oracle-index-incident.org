# Blast radius — the production index I dropped in my first sysadmin job

Use when asked about a failure, about production judgement, or about why the
author insists on plan-first and private spikes. This is the origin story, and
it is told without decoration.

* What happened

The first commercial Linux administration job: Seznam.cz, at a time when
**Postfix did not exist yet** — Sendmail and Qmail were the mail servers in the
building, and when a spam wave hit, the admins sat down and cleaned the queues by
hand.

The production Oracle database ran low on disk and the system became slow.
Under pressure — *"I have to do something, I have to do something, so what can
I do"* — he dropped an index to free space. He knew the data itself was safe.
What he did not know was the **cost of the operation**: rebuilding the index
takes most of a day (normally scheduled for the night). One department had a
slow system for a whole day. He was let go at the end of probation.

* What actually failed

Not the technical knowledge. The **risk assessment**:

- **Irreversibility.** The action was cheap to take and expensive to undo, and
  that asymmetry was never checked before the action.
- **Blast radius.** The cost did not fall on him; it fell on a third party (a
  department that could not work). This is the part he keeps naming.
- **The heuristic.** Under uncertainty the reflex was *"I'll do something and
  see what happens"* — a naive, risky move that he recognises in himself to
  this day and consciously guards against.

* The same week, the same person — the counter-example

The same period contains the opposite evidence. A database (Oracle or Sybase)
would not run because of system library incompatibilities (`libc.so` and
similar). Rather than shuffling library versions on the host, he tried
**chroot into an older stable Debian** — the binaries found the libraries they
expected, and it worked. It became the team's systematic solution.

Both outcomes have the **same source**: curiosity plus "let me try it". The
difference is not talent or caution — it is **containment**. In the chroot case
production was treated as a laboratory only inside an isolated directory; in
the index case an irreversible change was made directly on a production system.

That sentence is the whole object: *the trait that produces the good work is the
same trait that produced the failure; what differs is the boundary around it.*

* What it changed

The design rules this person now builds by did not come from a course, they
came from this:

- **plan before apply**, and read the plan as a risk document;
- **private spike** instead of experimenting on a shared system;
- **`prevent_destroy`** and resource-level destroy policies — an operation that
  cannot be undone must be impossible to trigger casually;
- **small, low-risk reproduction loops** on shared infrastructure
  (see [[id:20261008-cicd-jenkins-openshift][the mini-seed pattern]]);
- **immutable, versioned state** instead of in-place mutation
  (see [[id:20261017-infrastructure-guardrails][guardrails by construction]]).

* The culture he remembers — and why it matters

It was, in his words, a very pleasant place: an open space, senior Linux people
he admired, and when a spam attack came, everyone sat down at their machines
and worked the queues. The founder walked in to discuss the newest technology
trends **and asked the engineers for their opinion** — which, to a young admin,
felt strange and then felt like being part of a family.

That is also a data point about the environments he seeks: technical seniors to
learn from, and leadership that talks to engineers as peers.

* Honest self-assessment

He still calls it *"my fuck-up"* without softening it: dropping an index on a
production database was a real fuck-up. What he takes from it is not shame but a
named internal alarm: when he notices the "do something and see" pressure, that
is the moment to stop and check the blast radius. The failure mode is known,
named, and designed against — which is the only honest way to present it.
