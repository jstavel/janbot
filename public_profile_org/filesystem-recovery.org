# Filesystem forensics on a student's Linux — carving, superblocks, partition tables

Use when asked about Linux depth, incident composure, or "have you ever really
broken something". Two recoveries he can retrace step by step — both starting
from his own mistakes — and one rescue of somebody else's Windows machine where
the value was access to the data and the decision was left to its owner.

* Recovery 1 — deleted files, ext2

It started with his own `rm -rf` on a filesystem that mattered, with `rm`
aliased so that it never asked. He still remembers the heat of the moment.

The procedure, in this order:

1. **Remount the affected partition read-only immediately.** First action, before
   thinking further — every write can overwrite the freed blocks.
2. **grep the raw block device** (`/dev/hda6`, in the extended partition) for
   known text strings.
3. For each hit, extract a **chunk of bytes around the match** and collect them
   in a directory.
4. Reassemble the pieces by hand.

Honest attribution: the method was found on a blog, not invented — a tool or
procedure of that era, possibly related to the GParted family. What was his was
the decision order: **freeze first, search the raw device second, reassemble
third.** With ext3 and its journal the same class of recovery became much easier.

* Recovery 2 — lost superblock, partition not mountable

The partition would not mount; the primary superblock was gone.

1. **Take the disk completely offline**: boot a Live CD (Knoppix, later a
   Parted/GParted live image), attach a large external USB disk as the
   destination for anything recovered.
2. **grep the raw device for backup superblocks** — not for text, for metadata.
3. From the positions of the backup superblocks, **derive the original geometry**
   (cylinders, heads, offsets, partition start in blocks).
4. Write the **partition table back by hand in `parted`**, exactly to the computed
   geometry. No formatting, no `mkfs`, nothing that writes over the data, until
   the table is restored and verified.
5. Mount. The data was there.

He describes the moment the table worked as one he remembers to this day.

* Rescue 3 — NTFS: mount read-only, save the data, then reinstall

The value here was never a repair. It was **access to the data**, and a decision
left to the person who owned the machine.

The situation: in the early NTFS era, friends' Windows machines often ended up in
a state where the boot-time recovery/administrator mode offered very few options
and frequently did not solve the problem.

What he actually did:

1. Booted Linux from a CD.
2. Mounted the NTFS volume **read-only**.
3. Asked the owner the only question that mattered: *"is there anything you want
   to save?"*
4. Copied out what mattered.
5. The disk was then **reformatted and Windows reinstalled**. Nothing else was
   attempted.

No repair was performed, and he is clear that he does not believe he had the
tools for an NTFS repair at the time — which matches the era: the Linux NTFS
driver was read-only, safe write support arrived much later, and `ntfsfix` only
clears the dirty flag so that *Windows itself* runs `chkdsk`. Repairing an NTFS
volume from a Linux CD was simply not on the table.

**Why it was worth doing** — answering his own later doubt (*"it doesn't make
much sense, they could just boot their own CD"*): Windows setup and the recovery
console gave no way to reach files on a sick volume. A Linux CD did. So the
reinstall happened anyway, but **without data loss and without the risk of
formatting over something that mattered**. The benefit was real; it was just not
the benefit the first recollection claimed.

**The correction itself is part of the object.** The first version of this memory
said *"they didn't have to reinstall"*; the actual outcome was: data rescued,
disk reformatted, Windows reinstalled. What survived the years reliably was the
**scene** and the **act of asking before destroying** — the method and even the
outcome had been retro-fitted with later knowledge. This is the same failure mode
the corpus guards against elsewhere: a memory that becomes more coherent than the
event probably was.

Also from that period: cloning disks over the network in GParted.

* What it demonstrates

- **The filesystem is a data structure, not an appliance.** If the metadata is
  damaged, the data is still on the medium and can be found by searching for
  structure.
- **A safe order of operations beats a clever tool**: freeze writes, work on a
  copy or an offline device, rebuild metadata, verify, and only then mount.
- **The irreversible step belongs to the person who pays for it.** Before
  formatting someone else's disk he asked what they wanted to keep — the
  blast-radius reflex, exercised casually years before it was given that name
  and long before the production incident that made it a rule.
- **Comfort below the abstraction.** No layer of the stack is magic; it can be
  opened, read and reasoned about.
- The same pull as building a Linux From Scratch on a floppy
  ([[id:20261007-linux-from-scratch][LFS on 1.44MB]]) and the same lesson from the
  other side as the production index incident
  ([[id:20261017-oracle-index-incident][blast radius]]).

* Open question, recorded live

Where is the equivalent today? Kubernetes, containers and managed disks put the
raw device out of reach; the "geek operation" of the block-device era has no
obvious cloud-native counterpart. He names this as something he *wants to test*
in the new environment — a learning direction, not a claim of expertise.
