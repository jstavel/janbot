# AI Fast Failback — contributing a documentation-as-metric idea to a company-wide research initiative

Near the end of the author's tenure, a **company-wide research initiative**
was opened around a question about AI trust. Participants brought their own
ideas, and working groups formed around the ideas that were taken up — one
of them around the author's. Use to demonstrate: (1) thinking about AI
trust and verification before it was fashionable, (2) documentation treated
as a first-class test artifact, (3) the shift-left interface between tester
and developer.

* The idea the author brought

The initiative's research question — *can AI be trusted on pull
requests?* — was about **metrics**: which signals make it possible for
AI to judge whether a pull request is potentially safe or unsafe. The
author joined the initiative, and at the opening brainstorming meetings
contributed his answer, built from his testing experience:

1. **Code alone is not enough signal.** If AI is to judge a PR, it needs
   to read more than the diff — it needs to read the *documentation
   around the change*: the bug record, how to reproduce it, what to
   verify. A well-documented bug (simulation steps + validation steps)
   is the ground truth an automated judge can check a fix against.
2. **Documentation quality is therefore a PR-safety metric.** Documentation
   written so it can be *executed and simulated* makes the developer's life
   easier — they reproduce behaviour immediately — and gives the AI (or a
   human reviewer) something concrete to validate against.

The second point is the author's recurring signature: the same style as
Reactive Testing — describe clearly how to simulate, how to check, and
hand it to the developer. Verification described first, automation
added later.

* What happened — how the initiative worked

The shape of the initiative matters: it existed first, as a research
project. It was **not** created by the author's idea; it was *populated*
by it.

1. The initiative opened around its research question and held
   **introductory brainstorming meetings**.
2. People joined and **brought their own ideas**.
3. Out of the brainstorming, **several working groups formed — one around
   each idea that was taken up**.
4. One of those groups formed around the author's idea: making
   documentation quality (as captured in bug/Jira tickets) a measurable
   signal for AI PR-judgement.

His contribution was therefore: joining the initiative, bringing one idea
into the brainstorm at the opening meetings, and having that idea selected
and staffed as its own working group.

What followed:

- Working on it opened doors across the company: colleagues he had never
  met reached out offering artifacts — "I have an agent/MCP for Jira
  tickets, might be useful for you." A small network of AI-practitioner
  contacts formed around the initiative.
- The follow-on concept extended the pattern: write test intent as
  **behaviors** (business-readable, not strictly Gherkin), let **AI
  generate the test code** from them, and keep **verifications as a
  separate part** that starts as manual validation and is automated
  over time. The author explicitly recognized this as the same lineage
  as his earlier Ansible-based verification work.

* Why the metric belongs there — the author's core conviction

Stated plainly by the author, and worth quoting because it is the whole
argument in one sentence:

> If a bug is documented well — and it is described well *how to simulate
> it* and *what to check* — then the chance that the fix will be safe is
> much greater.

That is why documentation quality belongs in the metrics: documentation
is not paperwork around the bug, it is the **reproduction and validation
contract**. A fix is only as verifiable as the description it is judged
against. Measuring documentation quality therefore measures the
verifiability of the codebase — the precondition for AI failing fast on
a risky change.

* How the idea travelled

The initiative was recognized as valuable for the company ("what is good
for the company is good for the team" — the early framing). The point of
this object is not that the initiative existed, nor that anyone led it:
it is that an idea the author had been applying personally in his own
testing — simulate, then validate, described clearly enough for someone
else to run — was picked up out of a company-wide brainstorm and staffed
as a working group of its own.

A note on scope: the initiative was later de-scoped locally and the
author eventually stopped contributing to it. That belongs to the
departure narrative, not here. This object records the part that
matters — the idea, why it belongs in the metrics, and that it was
taken up beyond one person's desk. The author's own summary:

> "What pleased me was that the idea caught on — and I think it does
> belong in the metrics."
