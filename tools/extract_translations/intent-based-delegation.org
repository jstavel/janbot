# Delegate the intent, not the steps — and verify the output

Use this when someone asks how I work with an AI agent — or how I lead people who
understand their work better than I understand their steps.

* The correction I made to my own practice

In the JanBot project the executing agent knows the methodology (BMAD) and carries
the repository's context. Up to now, though, I still handed it precise
instructions: *do A, then B, then C.* From the outside that is **micromanagement**
— and it is not what I claim to do. The claim and the practice were diverging, and
that is the only reason fixing it was worth it.

The role I want is not the one that gives orders, but the **independent leader and
observer**: hold clean outputs, do not touch the internal process.

* What replaces it

| Micromanagement | Delegation by intent |
|-----------------|----------------------|
| *do A, then B* | *this is the change the output must cause* |
| the process is controlled by watching it | the process is checked at agreed points |
| quality is judged by resemblance to my process | quality is judged by acceptance criteria |
| deviation = disobedience | deviation = information (either my intent was unclear, or the method found something I could not see) |

The brief that gets handed over is therefore: **intent — definition of the done
state — constraints — autonomy — verification.** What happens inside that frame is
the executor's decision and responsibility.

The formulation is deliberately borrowed from *Turn the Ship Around* (L. David
Marquet): **trust, but verify.** Trust is what makes autonomy real; verification is
what makes it safe. Either without the other is an error state — trust without
verification is abdicating the steering, and verification without trust is the
micromanagement we started from.

* Three concrete damages of micromanagement

This is not an aesthetic objection; each of the three produces a concrete defect:

1. **Two authorities.** My step-by-step instruction competes with the methodology
   the executor is supposed to follow. When the two diverge, the executor must
   choose which master to obey — and that choice is invisible to me.
2. **An executor instead of an engineer.** The executor stops reporting conflicts
   with convention, because reporting is not in the instruction. The most valuable
   signal — *"this step you gave me contradicts the method"* — disappears exactly
   when it is needed most.
3. **Drift when the methodology changes.** My instruction does not age together
   with the method. It stays in the record as a rule even after the methodology it
   was derived from has moved on — and the next person (or the next session)
   inherits it as if it still held.

* Verification is against the intent, not against the instruction

The check after delivery is not *"it did what I told it"* — that would smuggle
micromanagement back into the review. The questions are:

- does the result match the problem I posed?
- are the claims evidenced (not merely asserted)?
- did a silent drift occur — did something change that nobody reported?
- do the decisions the executor made stand on their own?

When it was my intent that was wrong, that is a finding about me, not about the
executor. The signal worth pursuing is the solution that exposes a defective brief;
that is the moment to say so — not the moment to correct the direction.

* Honest limits

- Observed on **one agent, in one project**. The principle comes from a submarine
  crew, not from a team I led — the transfer to people is an expectation, not a
  result.
- Without acceptance criteria written in advance, "verify the output" falls back
  into a question of taste. The template (intent / done / constraints / autonomy /
  verification) is described here, but is not yet in routine use.
- Autonomy without an explicitly stated risk budget produces either a stuck
  executor (asking permission for everything) or an overshooting one. That
  boundary must be part of the intent, not something discovered after the fact.
